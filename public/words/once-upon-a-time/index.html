Once Upon a Time

Nov 26, 2026 • 19:07 / 4 min read
Table of contents

Once upon a time, a boy wrote a great deal. He wrote every thought and day dream. He wrote flash stories, short stories, poetry, poorly written but passionate essays. The boy lived in his thoughts and he thought a great deal about a great deal until one day he stopped.

I couldn’t tell you why. If, as I keep saying, how you write is how you think, then I haven’t done much writing since, and so I haven’t done much thinking since. I haven’t explored why it stopped. I don’t know what I have learned or entirely what was lost from that. What I do know is something has certainly been lost.

The words don’t come as easily. No matter what I try to write, it doesn’t come without friction. It takes effort and every sentence is ultimately unsatisfying. I neither have the discipline nor the instinct for good writing. I find myself agonizing over how to simplify a sentence, or whether I have used too many commas or not enough. I struggle to organize what I’m trying to say or where I want to go with it.

It has never felt this difficult. I can fire off jargon-filled corporate email and fill up bullet points and PowerPoints. I have endless pithy re-tweets and memes, and I’m not above a classic wall of texts when my fingers are having an ADHD moment. But I cannot just write clearly and take command of how I communicate. I can’t be focused or serious or funny. My metaphor game is whack. My analogies are tortured, and anything figurative is literally a dead end.

Even my day dreams have suffered. Uninteresting power fantasies that powerscale to godhood where I immediately get bored and go look for more work email to chew on or a Youtube home feed to refresh. In this chapter I have become somehow so much more and so much less. So much more in what I’ve seen and done and conquered. So much less in that I’ve lost my ability to mentally digest it all and wrestle with it on paper (or screen). I no longer “talk so well”, I just talk and drown myself in the sound of my own voice.

But there is something there. A kernel of something. I am not dead, just stagnant and unpracticed. The thing is I know the cure. The steps are like any other exercise. First I acknowledge that:

  • Nothing I write for a long time will be any good.
  • Even as it improves, I will hate it for much longer than it stays bad.
  • Long after I consistently notice the problems, I will be too lazy to fix it for a while.
  • There is no cure to this stagnation than to just do the thing.

I first need to write more. I need to write a lot more. I need to jot things on notebooks, and turn phrases into sentences. I need to just write and write more and wrestle with my sense of inadequacy.

Then in time I can start to edit, trim, clean up and correct. Don’t stress over how many commas, just write as many commas as I feel I need. Then in time when I feel moved to do so, I can actually look up the rules, remind myself, and use my own writing to practice. In time I will care more about the quality of my writing, and I will ride the balance until I have to start sacrificing the initial quantity for more quality.

As I get into it, I will start to look up more things, more rules, perhaps imitate things I read and chew on what I like or dislike about them. Like a sedentary person who starts with a daily short stroll until they walk faster and longer, then one day they find the energy to do some simple thing more. Before they know it, they become an active person.

Will I always have worthy material? No. Absolutely not. But I have day after day of novelty. Lessons to chew on, textbook concepts to meditate on, logs of my day and projects, things I read and could take the time to think through what I liked or disliked about them, and on and on. And failing all that, I could find writing prompts if I must. I could transcribe my scribbled pocket notes and stretch them out. Available material is not the bottleneck. My on will is.

So once upon a time, a boy wrote a great deal. Then he stopped. Then an old man picked up a pen. Then sighed and put it down because the screen was not a touch screen. Then he wondered whether he had time for a touch typing class to save himself from the tedium of typing too slow. Then noticed the time and how late he was running. Then finally had to admit he just didn’t know how to end the thing or whether this is a good spot for an ellipsis…
